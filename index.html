The idea

What exactly is a universal basic income?

In its plain form, UBI is a regular cash payment to every adult: no income test, no work requirement, the same amount for a CEO and a caregiver. It is meant as a floor, not a ceiling. Several of the plans on this site bend one of those rules, which is where the arguments start.

The idea is about 400 years old and has supporters across the political spectrum: libertarian economists who want to replace the welfare bureaucracy, labor leaders who want unpaid work recognized, and tech founders who expect AI to displace jobs. It has critics on the left and right too.

The evidence so far

It has been tested. The results are mixed.

Dozens of experiments in the US and abroad have given people cash with no conditions. None has tested a permanent, nationwide payment, so each tells you part of the story. Here are three of the most significant.

🇺🇸

Alaska Permanent Fund

Every Alaskan has received an annual oil dividend since 1982, with no conditions. It is the longest-running program of its kind, though at $1,000 to $2,000 a year it is a supplement, not an income.

15–25k residents kept out of poverty each year ISER
🇫🇮

Finland Pilot

2,000 unemployed citizens received €560/month for two years with no conditions. A government-run, peer-reviewed randomized trial.

↑ Wellbeing less stress, more trust, equal employment Finnish Ministry
🇺🇸

OpenResearch Study

1,000 low-income Americans received $1,000 a month for 3 years in the largest randomized US trial, funded in part by Sam Altman. Spending on basics rose; recipients also worked about 1.3 hours less a week.

+$169/mo more on food, rent & transport OpenResearch NBER

The hardest question

Who pays for it?

This is where the plans really differ. A payment of $1,000 a month to every adult costs about $3.1 trillion a year, close to half of all federal spending. Every plan has to say where that comes from, and each answer has different winners and losers.

Common objections

Five objections, and what each side says

These are the questions every basic income plan has to answer. On most of them the evidence does not settle the matter.

"People will just stop working."+

Supporters point to: In Stockton, full-time employment among recipients rose from 28% to 40%. SEED In Finland, employment matched the control group. Finnish Ministry

Critics point to: In the largest US trial, recipients worked about 1.3 hours less a week and were 2 points less likely to be employed. NBER No pilot has tested a permanent payment, where the effect could be larger.

"It would cause inflation."+

Supporters point to: A payment funded by taxes moves money around without creating more of it, so total spending power barely changes.

Critics point to: Even without new money, shifting spending toward lower-income households could push up prices where supply is tight, such as rent. Plans funded by borrowing would add to demand directly.

"We can't afford it."+

Supporters point to: The sticker price overstates the burden, because most people pay taxes in and get the payment back. Targeted versions cost a small fraction of universal ones. Some models find growth offsets part of the cost. Roosevelt Institute

Critics point to: The best-known universal plan was found to be about half funded, and to slow growth slightly instead of speeding it up. Tax Foundation Closing the gap means much higher taxes or a smaller payment.

"People will waste it."+

Supporters point to: In Stockton the top spending categories were food, utilities and car repairs. Across 19 studies, cash transfers did not increase spending on alcohol and tobacco. Evans & Popova

Critics point to: Most of that evidence comes from short pilots and lower-income countries. Critics also argue that services such as healthcare and housing aid guarantee outcomes that cash cannot.

"It's a left-wing idea." Or: "It's a right-wing trick."+

Supporters point to: Both have a point. Milton Friedman proposed a version to shrink the welfare state Friedman, and Martin Luther King Jr. proposed one to end poverty.

Critics point to: That is the reason to ask which plan. A basic income that replaces existing programs and one added on top of them are opposite policies with the same name.
