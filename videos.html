These five people want to give you money

A civil rights leader, a free-market economist, a presidential candidate and two AI executives all landed on the same idea: guarantee every American some income. They disagree on almost everything else.

The idea
A regular cash payment with no work requirement
Who backs it
Left, right, libertarian and tech
The real fight
Who gets it, how much, and who pays
This series
One episode per plan, each making its best case

The case for it

Poverty, stagnant pay and automation are three different problems, and a cash floor is one of the few answers that has been proposed for all three. That is why people who agree on nothing else keep arriving at it. History

The strongest objection

"Basic income" hides plans that differ by a factor of ten in cost and by who ends up paying. Some of its best-known backers have hedged or moved on. And the largest US trial found modest results, not a transformation. NBER

In production The conservative plan to give poor people cash Working title · Episode 1

Episode 1 · Milton Friedman · 1962

The conservative plan to give poor people cash

Friedman wanted to replace the welfare system with one payment through the tax code: if you earn below a set line, the IRS pays you, and the payment shrinks as your income rises.

Who gets it
Households below an income line
How much
A floor near the poverty line, fading out at 50 cents per dollar earned
Who pays
General taxes, offset by ending existing aid programs
Price tag
About $219B a year (2015 estimate)

The case for it

It costs a fraction of a universal payment because money only goes where income is low. It never punishes work with a benefit cliff: every extra dollar earned leaves you better off. And it swaps dozens of agencies for a line on a tax return. MIT Sloan

The strongest objection

The phase-out works like a 50% tax on the earnings of the poorest workers, and the 1970s experiments found people did work somewhat less. It depends on tax filing, which misses many of the people it is meant for. Replacing food and housing aid with cash leaves some households worse off. Moffitt, NBER CBPP

In production MLK's last big idea: a guaranteed income Working title · Episode 2

Episode 2 · Martin Luther King Jr. · 1967

MLK's last big idea: a guaranteed income

In his final book King argued that the way to end poverty was to end it directly, with a guaranteed income tied to the median American income and rising as the country grew richer.

Who gets it
Everyone, alongside a push for full employment
How much
Pegged to median income, not the poverty line
Who pays
Not specified. He pointed to war and space spending
Price tag
$6.2T a year at $2,000 a month, the most this calculator shows

The case for it

King saw housing, schooling and jobs programs each attacking one symptom of poverty while leaving people poor. Income attacks the cause. Pegging it to the median was the point: a floor set at the bottom, he warned, would freeze people there. King, 1967

The strongest objection

It is by far the most expensive version, and King left the funding to others. The estimate he cited, $20 billion a year, was for a far smaller program than a median-level income. At that level, economists expect large effects on work and prices that no pilot has tested.

In production $1,000 a month, paid for by a sales tax Working title · Episode 3

Episode 3 · Andrew Yang · 2019

$1,000 a month, paid for by a sales tax

Yang ran for president on a Freedom Dividend: $1,000 a month to every adult citizen, funded mostly by a 10% value-added tax of the kind most rich countries already have.

Who gets it
Every adult citizen. People on benefits choose one or the other
How much
$1,000 a month
Who pays
Everyone who spends, plus traders, polluters and high earners
Price tag
$2.8T a year (Tax Foundation); $3.1T for all adults here

The case for it

A value-added tax is hard to dodge and reaches companies that pay little income tax. On its own it hits the poor hardest, but pair it with a flat $1,000 and roughly anyone spending under $120,000 a year comes out ahead. It is the most fully specified plan of the five. Yang 2020

The strongest objection

The numbers did not close. The Tax Foundation found the taxes would raise about $1.3 trillion, under half the cost, and that the economy would shrink slightly instead of delivering the $800 billion in growth Yang counted on. Making it balance would take a 22% VAT and a $750 payment. Tax Foundation

In production What if you owned a piece of AI? Working title · Episode 4

Episode 4 · Sam Altman · 2021 and 2026

What if you owned a piece of AI?

Altman's answer to AI taking jobs is ownership instead of a check: a national fund that holds a slice of every large company and of all private land, with every adult as a shareholder.

Who gets it
Every adult, as an owner of the fund
How much
$13,500 a year by his 2021 estimate, a decade out
Who pays
Large companies (2.5% of value a year, in shares) and landowners (2.5%)
Status
In 2026 he said he believes in cash UBI less than he did

The case for it

If AI pushes the value of work down and the value of capital up, taxing wages funds less every year. Owning capital does the opposite: the payout grows as AI does. And shareholders, unlike welfare recipients, have a stake in the system doing well. Altman, 2021

The strongest objection

At today's values the two levies cover about half of his own figure; the rest depends on growth that has not happened yet. A share can lose value exactly when people need it. Altman funded the largest US cash trial, then said in 2026 that a fixed payment is not what is needed, which leaves the plan without a firm version. Business Insider

In production The AI CEO who wants his company taxed Working title · Episode 5

Episode 5 · Dario Amodei · 2025 and 2026

The AI CEO who wants his company taxed

The head of Anthropic has warned that AI could wipe out a large share of entry-level office jobs, and says that if the loss is permanent, the companies building AI should be taxed to support the people displaced.

Who gets it
Not specified. Framed as support if job loss is large and lasting
How much
No amount named
Who pays
AI companies, or investors through higher capital gains taxes
Status
A contingency, after wage insurance and incentives to keep staff

The case for it

It ties the bill to the cause. If AI replaces work, AI revenue is where the money will be, and a tax on it grows automatically with the problem. It also comes from someone arguing against his own company's interest. Bloomberg Tax

The strongest objection

At the reported 3% rate, today's AI revenue would raise a few billion dollars against a cost in the trillions. The plan only works if AI revenue grows more than tenfold, and it names no amount, no rate and no trigger. Forecasts of mass job loss have been wrong before, and US unemployment was 4.3% when he published. Tech Times
