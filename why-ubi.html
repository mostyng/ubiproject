Four arguments, and what the other side says

People reach basic income from different directions. Each argument below is stated the way its supporters would make it, next to the strongest reply. Which ones persuade you says a lot about which plan you would back.

Argument one

The country is rich enough to end poverty.

The case

Lifting every American to the poverty line would take about $288 billion a year, roughly 1% of GDP and about a third of the defense budget. Federal Safety Net 35.9 million people were below that line in 2024. US Census

Supporters say the obstacle is design, not money. Aid is split across dozens of programs with separate rules, cliffs and paperwork, and many eligible people never get it. Cash sent directly skips all of that.

The counterpoint

$288 billion is the cost of a perfectly targeted payment that tops up only the poor. A payment to everyone costs about ten times as much, because most of it goes to people who are not poor and has to be taxed back.

Existing programs also do more than the official poverty rate shows, since that measure leaves out most non-cash aid and tax credits. Critics argue a universal payment funded by cutting them could leave the poorest worse off. CBPP

Closest plans: Friedman takes the targeted route. King rejects the poverty line as the goal.

Argument two

Workers produced more than they were paid for.

The case

From 1948 to the early 1970s, pay rose in step with productivity. Since 1979 productivity is up about 90% and typical pay about 33%. Economic Policy Institute

On this view a basic income is back pay: a partial return of gains that went to shareholders and executives through weaker unions, trade and tax policy.

The counterpoint

The size of the gap depends on how you measure it. Using average instead of typical pay, and the same inflation measure on both lines, shrinks it considerably. Stansbury and Summers find productivity growth still raises pay; other forces have held typical pay down. NBER

And a payment to every adult is a blunt way to repay workers: it goes equally to people who never lost out. Critics who accept the gap often prefer raising wages directly, through bargaining power or a higher minimum wage.

Closest plans: King, and the progressive case generally. Nobody in the series funds a plan this way.

Argument three

AI will take jobs faster than people can retrain.

The case

A 2013 Oxford study put 47% of US jobs at high risk of automation. Oxford Martin In 2025 the head of Anthropic warned AI could eliminate half of entry-level office jobs within five years. Axios

Federal retraining programs have a weak record. GAO Supporters argue that if work stops being a reliable source of income for many people, income has to come from somewhere else, and the wealth automation creates is the obvious source.

The counterpoint

Predictions of mass technological unemployment are as old as the loom and have not come true yet. The OECD, looking at tasks instead of whole occupations, put the share of highly automatable jobs at 14%, and about 10% for the US. Frey & Osborne on the numbers

If displacement turns out to be temporary or concentrated, a permanent payment to everyone is the wrong tool; wage insurance or targeted support would cost far less. Even Amodei treats basic income as a last step, not a first one.

Closest plans: Altman and Amodei, who both fund their answer from AI itself. Yang ran on this argument.

Argument four

A floor gives people room to choose.

The case

Many Americans cannot cover a $1,000 emergency from savings, and about one in four did gig or contract work in 2024, mostly without benefits. ADP Research

Supporters argue that a guaranteed floor changes what people can say no to: a bad job, an unsafe workplace, a wage that does not cover rent. It also pays, in effect, for work the market ignores, such as caring for family, and gives people without savings a way to study or start something.

The counterpoint

The best US evidence is mixed. In the largest randomized trial, people receiving $1,000 a month worked about 1.3 hours less a week and were 2 points less likely to be employed, and researchers found no clear improvement in the quality of their jobs. NBER

Whether that is a cost or the point depends on your values: time moved to leisure and caregiving. Some labor advocates worry about the opposite risk, that a floor lets employers pay less and gives politicians an excuse to cut worker protections.

Closest plans: Yang and King. See the pilot results for the evidence in detail.
